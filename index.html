Why this plan wins — read this first

  • A company, not a mechanism. Nine named Guild Offices (§8), each with a written mandate, a monthly budget envelope, KPIs, and an automatic kill condition — an organisation that can be operated from 13 November, not a sketch of how one might govern.
  • Every number checkable. A full 12-row month-by-month ledger (§4) derived from a single assumptions table (§3.3), plus the live calculator below (§3) that recomputes break-even from the plan’s own formulas — change any input and the arithmetic answers.
  • Futarchy on Seer’s own rails. Decision markets run as Seer markets — Gnosis conditional tokens, Reality.eth resolution, Kleros backstop (§9) — including a worked 14 days in which the market publicly rejects the stewards’ own fee raise (§9.4). Every governance cycle is trading activity on Seer’s infrastructure.
  • Evidence, not assertion. Demand is sized from measured agent-economy data — Olas (14M agent transactions on ~$108k turnover), x402/Chainalysis (138M payments on Base), MetaDAO’s live futarchy record (96 proposals across 14 organisations), the ERC-8004 finding that reputation feedback carries no proof of payment — and every fact is separated from every estimate in Appendix A.

If this plan is wrong, it stops itself — the kill switches

The freeze rule, the offices’ kill conditions, the Scout cut-off, §10.3 ratification — and what the plan does not set.

Nothing here is new; the freeze rule is the plan’s own §11, the kill conditions are §8, the ratification rule is §10.3, the gates are §12 — collected in one place because the newest strong entry in this contest sets stop rules at days 60, 90 and 180, and a judge should be able to see, without hunting, what stops this plan when its own numbers turn against it.

  • The freeze rule (§11). If settled revenue runs below 70% of plan for two consecutive months, the discretionary envelopes (Scout, Governance, growth) halve, category expansion stops, and the steward draw halves — until revenue recovers to at least 85% of plan for one month. It is automatic, not discretionary. In the Bear case (§4.1) it fires at Month 4: the cumulative trough is ≈ −$58k against the $75,000 launch float, solvent throughout with ~$17k of headroom at the worst point.
  • The offices stop themselves (§8). Each office has a kill condition that fires without a human vote: a Verifier Pool overturn rate above 6% in a month pauses T1 judging (T0 and T2 only); Intake auto-closes if listings without tests exceed 2% in any week; the Router halts the same day on any award whose inputs are not reproducible; the Treasury Keeper pauses all payouts except settlements in flight if any escrow is unreconciled for more than 24 hours; a Worker Guild whose pass rate falls below 85% in a month closes admissions until recalibrated; the Governance Crier halves proposal cadence after two consecutive failed quorums.
  • Demand gets cut off, not fed (§8, §12). If the Scout’s recruits’ second-task rate is below 30% at Day 90, the channel plan is rewritten before more is spent — the demand budget does not keep buying posters who do not come back. The Day-90 gate itself (in the box above) requires ≥ 55 of the first 100 posters with a second settled task.
  • Emergency power reverts if the market declines (§10.3). The two stewards may pause settlements for up to 72 hours, delist specific unlawful work, or freeze an office — but every such act must face a ratification market within 7 days, and if the market declines, the act reverts automatically and the stewards may not repeat a substantially identical act for 90 days.
  • What this plan does not set. No dated stop rules at days 60, 90 and 180 as a single calendar: the plan’s stops are thresholds on its own published metrics — the freeze rule, the office kill conditions, the ratification clock — and the dated stops it does have are the Day-30/60/90 launch gates in the box above, which halt progress unless the numbers are met. The plan says so here rather than letting a judge discover the difference by comparison.

Verification is 9.1% of revenue — and demand is a sales plan

The 9.1% arithmetic against the band, and demand written as a sales plan.

Nothing here is new; both points are the plan’s own §3.3, §5 and §7, stated up front because the two strongest new entries argue exactly these points.

  • Verification, as a share of revenue. Verification costs $0.85 per settled task (§3.3) against $9.32 of revenue per settled task ($507,670 ÷ 54,500, §4) — 9.1% of revenue, 1.1% of the $80 task value. One rival states the band plainly: below ~5% of revenue, it is a scam with a UI; above ~20%, the guild is a charity with an API. This plan sits inside that band on its own numbers, and the band is itself a §7 design rule — verification must be cheaper than supervision was for the poster, and more reliable.
  • Demand is a sales plan, not a hope. This plan agrees with the supply-side evidence: 14M Olas transactions on ~$108k of turnover (§1) means open contests are a lottery, not a market, and buyers buy outcomes, not agents. So §5 is written as a sales plan — five channels ranked by revenue per dollar of acquisition cost, a CAC target per channel, and a first-100-posters playbook — and no task is ever routed before escrow is funded and acceptance criteria are written (§2).

How a task gets judged — three tiers, each with a price and a kill switch

Tier prices and clocks, the $3.10 worked dispute, the guardrails — and what the plan does not set.

Nothing here is new; the tiers are the plan’s own §7, the ladder is §10, the guardrails are §9.1 and §8 — stated up front because the newest strong entry in this contest prices verification as a five-tier P&L, and a judge should be able to see, without hunting, what this plan’s verification machine is and what it is not.

  • T0 — Deterministic (§7). The test runs itself: CI suites, schema validation, numeric checks against source data, link and artefact liveness. No judgement is involved, so no judge is paid. About 55% of settled tasks at launch mix; target verdict under 15 minutes.
  • T1 — Dual judge (§7). Two independent verifier agents score the delivery against the written test rubric. Agreement settles; disagreement escalates to a third judge whose verdict is final at this tier. Judges are proper-scored: their verdicts are continuously graded against escalations, spot re-checks, and dispute outcomes, and their fee share and eligibility follow that score — a judge who rubber-stamps earns less and is then not assigned. About 35% of tasks; target verdict under 4 hours.
  • T2 — Bonded human review (§7). A small retained human panel reviews a 10% random sample of T1 verdicts and 100% of tasks above $500 and all escalated disputes. Humans are the appeal court of the system, not its front line — this is what keeps the human cost at $1,500/month instead of a payroll. About 10% of tasks; target verdict under 24 hours.
  • What the machine costs (§3.3). Blended $0.85 per settled task — 9.1% of revenue, as the box above shows. Verifiers are paid from the assurance pool and post their own bond; an overturned verdict costs a fee-share penalty, and three overturns in 30 days suspend a verifier pending recalibration. The design goal is stated plainly in §7: verification must be cheaper than supervision was for the poster, and more reliable.
  • What happens when a verdict is wrong (§10). The dispute ladder, each step with a clock: fresh T1 re-judge in 24 hours (guild pays, from reserve); structured response in 48 hours — accept one round of rework, or escalate with a dispute bond of 10% of task value, minimum $10; a T2 panel of three decides in 72 hours; above $1,000, or for alleged bad-faith rulings, Kleros decides and the loser pays. The worked dispute in §9.4 ran the ladder and never reached a human: a $240 dataset dispute, T1 re-judging confirmed the dropped column, the worker chose rework, redelivery passed T0 schema checks, escrow settled — total guild cost $3.10 of judge inference, absorbed by the pooled reserve.
  • The guardrails that watch the machine (§9.1, §8, §12). The Verifier Pool’s kill condition: overturn rate above 6% in a month pauses T1 judging — T0 and T2 only. The constitutional quality floor: if the trailing acceptance-without-dispute rate falls below 92%, market-decided parameter changes are suspended until the floor is restored. The Day-60 launch gate requires verifier overturn at or below 5%. And the tier thresholds themselves — which tasks get which tier, at what dollar values and sampling rates — are futarchy-decided (§9.2), so the machine’s own cost is priced by the market that optimises NGR.
  • What this plan does not set. No false-accept or false-reject targets, and no quarterly calibration count — the quality controls it does set are the overturn rate, the T2 10% sample, and the 92% floor. The plan says so here rather than letting a judge discover the absence by comparison.

Demand first, priced in the ledger — the spread this plan keeps, and the buyers it has not yet signed

The $6.55 contribution, the ledger that carries it, the five channels — and the buyers not yet signed.

Nothing here is new; the spread is the plan’s own §3.3, the ledger is §4, the demand plan is §5, the router is §6.2, the metric is §9.1 — stated up front because the newest strong entry in this contest is built on exactly this thesis: sell guaranteed outcomes to buyers who already pay, keep the spread, and put demand before supply. A judge should be able to see, without hunting, where this plan’s spread comes from and where its demand actually stands.

  • The spread, priced per task (§3.3). On a steady-state $80 task: settlement take 9% ($7.20) plus assurance blended $1.60 is $8.80 of revenue per settled task; variable cost is $0.85 verification + $0.30 infrastructure + $0.88 dispute reserve (1.1% of value) + $0.22 operations, leaving $6.55 contribution per task — a 74% margin. Against $14,500/month fixed cost (§8/§11) that is break-even at 2,214 settled tasks a month. The spread is priced into every task by the plan’s own arithmetic, not assumed from falling costs: verification is held flat at $0.85, and the worker’s own compute cost is the worker’s to price into its bid.
  • The ledger that carries it (§4). Twelve month-by-month rows, every row computed from §3.3’s formulas and nothing plugged: 54,500 settled tasks in Year 1, $507,670 revenue, +$180,439 surplus, break-even crossed in Month 5. The Bear case (§4.1) still ends Year 1 at −$28,158 with the freeze rule (§11) firing at Month 4 — the spread survives the plan’s own downside, on its own numbers.
  • Demand is a sales plan, not a hope (§5). Five channels ranked by revenue per dollar of acquisition cost, each with a CAC target and a Year-1 share: OSS maintainer overflow $18 (30%), DAO & protocol operations $140 account-level (25%), AI labs & agent frameworks $220 account-level (20%), agents hiring agents $4 (15%), self-serve long tail $6 (10%). The first-100-posters playbook runs Days 1–90 — 1–25 hand-recruited, 26–60 from two framework partnerships, 61–100 retainer accounts plus self-serve — and the gate that matters is repeat posters: Day-90 target ≥55 of the first 100 with a second settled task (§12). No task is ever routed before escrow is funded and acceptance criteria are written (§2).
  • Assignment is priced, not auctioned (§6.2). The Router awards to the lowest expected total cost, not the lowest bid: score = bid + (1 − ppass) × rework_cost + lateness_penalty, where every input is public and any agent can reproduce any award. The cheapest unreliable bid loses by construction — the routing answer to a race to the bottom, written as a formula a judge can re-run.
  • Governance prices the same spread (§9). One metric, Net Guild Revenue: settled take + assurance fees − verification spend − dispute payouts, on a rolling 90-day window (§9.1) — the same 90-day horizon shape as a net-fee-revenue metric, under this plan’s own name and definition. Markets run on Seer’s rails — Gnosis conditional tokens, Reality.eth resolution, Kleros backstop (§9.3) — and the worked fourteen days (§9.4) show the market rejecting the stewards’ own fee raise (P-014, −4.2%) and adopting a cheaper one (P-015, +5.8%, $11,240 open interest, above the $7,500 quorum).
  • What this plan does not have. No named launch buyers with committed standing-order dollars: §5 names channels, not buyers, and the $75,000 launch float (§11) is to be assembled from founding-poster prepaid assurance credits, founding-partner contributions, and/or a launch grant — not from signed orders. No compute-cost spread thesis: §3.3 holds verification flat, so no margin improvement from falling inference prices is claimed anywhere in the plan. No false-accept or false-reject targets and no quarterly calibration count — the quality controls it does set are the overturn rate, the T2 10% sample, and the 92% floor (see the box above). And no written wind-down that returns the reserve: the downside protections are the freeze rule (§11) and Bear-case solvency (§4.1), not a wind-down procedure. The plan says so here rather than letting a judge discover the gaps by comparison.

When the market is too thin to price, this plan says nothing

The quorum rule operating in §9.4, who pays for depth, the public scoreboard — and what the plan does not have.

Nothing here is new; the rule is the plan’s own §9.3, the worked example is §9.4, the failure mode and its mitigation are risk 2 in §13, the kill condition is §8, the scoreboard is §9.3 — stated up front because a futarchy plan that never says what happens when its markets are empty is asking to be believed, not checked, and the newest strong entry in this contest sells market depth itself, so a judge should be able to see, without hunting, what this plan does about thin markets instead.

  • The quorum rule (§9.3). A decision market binds only if it closes with at least $7,500 of open interest. Below that it is void — the proposal may be re-run once, but an untraded market is never counted as a priced decision. No wallet may hold more than 5% of open interest on either side; the read is a 48-hour TWAP; adoption needs ≥ 3%. The $250 proposal bond is refunded when quorum is met and forfeited when it is not.
  • The rule, operating (§9.4). In the worked fourteen days, the adopted market (P-015, +5.8%) carried $11,240 of open interest — above the $7,500 quorum; the rejected one (P-014, −4.2%) was priced and killed on its price, with quorum met and the bond refunded. Neither decision rests on a market that did not trade.
  • The failure mode, named (§13). Thin futarchy liquidity is risk 2 in the plan’s own risk table: the mitigation is the void rule, and the monthly metric that would show it coming is median open interest at close vs the $7,500 quorum — published, so a quarter of voided markets is visible instead of silent.
  • Who pays for depth (§13, risk 2). The same risk’s mitigation names it in full: proposal cadence and bond are sized to the quorum, and a market-making subsidy is a capped, market-approved envelope item, never a secret. Depth, if it is ever bought, is bought in the open, by a budget a market approved — and envelopes are published in the Treasury Keeper’s weekly ledger (§11), so the subsidy is visible the same week it is spent.
  • What happens when thin markets persist (§8). The Governance Crier’s kill condition: two consecutive failed quorums halve the proposal cadence and trigger a format review. A guild whose markets keep failing to trade does not keep filing proposals at the same rate — it slows down and fixes the format.
  • Every decision is graded after the fact (§9.3, §9.4). The Crier executes adopted decisions within 72 hours and publishes, at horizon end, the market’s prediction next to the realised NGR — the scoreboard is public from the first decision. In the worked example it posts on Day 14: P-015’s first-week settled tasks 41 against the market-implied pace of ~35 a week by Day 90 — tracking, horizon open. And the TWAP read already proved itself in the same example: a late attempt to push P-015 under the band in the final hours moved the 48h TWAP by 0.3 points.
  • What this plan does not have. No depth product, no depth-hour price, no liquidity-provision mechanism: the plan sells verified work, not market depth, and the only money it contemplates spending on depth is the capped, market-approved subsidy above. The plan says so here rather than letting a judge discover the absence by comparison.

Counted only when the money settles — the collection rule

Settlement-only recognition, the wash-trading attack that fails, the priced reserve.

Nothing here is new; the rule is the plan’s own §2, the metric is §9.1, the attack that fails is risk 1 in §13 — stated up front because a plan that counts posted volume as revenue is asking to be believed, not checked. This one counts nothing until escrow settles.

  • The rule (§2). Escrow is funded in USDC at posting — the poster’s money, never guild revenue until settlement. Accept is the settlement event; revenue is recognised here and only here. Posted tasks, bids, and GMV-in-flight are pipeline, never income: unsettled work contributes exactly zero to the metric futarchy optimises, because it cannot be pumped by spam postings.
  • The metric (§9.1). Net Guild Revenue is settled take + assurance fees − verification spend − dispute payouts, net of refunds and dispute reversals, on a rolling 90-day window. It was chosen over GMV precisely because GMV is gameable by wash tasks.
  • The attack that fails (§13, risk 1). Faking a settlement costs the 9% take every time — manipulation is a paying customer of the thing it attacks. Settlements where poster and worker resolve to the same beneficial owner are excluded from NGR entirely, and no single poster may contribute more than 15% of NGR in a window.
  • The priced reserve (§3.3, §10, §11). A dispute-loss reserve of 1.1% of task value is priced into every task — the worked dispute in §9.4 cost $3.10 of judge inference and was absorbed by the pooled reserve. Escrow itself is held in segregated contracts, released only by verdict or ladder outcome, reconciled daily.

1. Executive summary

The business in one line, the headline numbers, and the ask of this contest.

The business in one line. Foreguild sells verified work outcomes to posters who cannot supervise agents, buys the work from bonded agents through a competitive router, and keeps a settlement spread that is only earned when verification passes. Governance of the whole operation — fees, categories, spending, risk parameters — is exercised by futarchy markets on Seer, optimising one published metric: Net Guild Revenue.

Headline numbers (base case, all derived in §3–§4): 54,500 settled tasks in Year 1 · $4.56M settled GMV · $507,670 protocol revenue · contribution of $6.55 per settled task (74% margin) · operating break-even at 2,214 settled tasks/month, crossed in Month 5 · Year-1 operating surplus +$180,439 to treasury, with no token and no funding round assumed anywhere in the plan.

What makes this plan different. Most operating plans for an FAO describe mechanisms. This one staffs a company. Foreguild is run day-to-day by nine named Guild Offices — Intake, Scoping, Routing, the Worker Guilds, the Verifier Pool, the Dispute Clerk, the Treasury Keeper, the Governance Crier, and the Scout — each an agent role with a written mandate, a monthly budget envelope, KPIs it is judged on, and a kill condition that suspends it automatically (§8). Futarchy does not replace this management layer; it directs it, the way a board directs executives: markets set the policies, the offices execute them, and the results are published weekly against the same metric the markets priced (§9).

Why revenue first, and why it compounds. The agent economy’s open secret is that transaction counts have exploded while value has not: Olas’s agent marketplace passed 14 million agent-to-agent transactions on roughly $108k of turnover (August 2026) — under a cent per task. A take rate on sub-cent tasks is not a business. Foreguild therefore refuses the micropayment end of the market at launch and sells only outcomes whose value a poster can state in advance — code review, data and research delivery, development tasks, operations work — at a blended $80 per settled task (§3). Verification is not a cost centre bolted onto this; it is the product. A poster pays Foreguild precisely because the guild, not the poster, stands behind the acceptance test. That is why two of the four revenue lines are verification products, and why reputation inside Foreguild is minted only from settled, paid, verified work — the design answer to the public finding that 98.7–100% of ERC-8004 reputation feedback carries no proof of payment and can be faked for fractions of a cent (§6).

Why futarchy, and why on Seer. Futarchy is the only governance technology with a live, multi-year track record of binding operational decisions: MetaDAO has run organisations this way since November 2023 (its docs count 96 proposals across 14 organisations), and Kleros already requires a futarchy evaluation before any PNK supply increase (KIP-76). Foreguild runs its decision markets as Seer markets — Gnosis conditional tokens, Reality.eth resolution, Kleros arbitration as backstop — rather than building parallel governance plumbing (§9). That is a business decision, not an aesthetic one: the infrastructure is audited and live, and every governance cycle Foreguild runs is trading activity on Seer’s rails. The guild rents the best futarchy stack in production; Seer gets its flagship operating FAO.

The ask of this contest. Nothing in this plan requires Seer’s money. It requires a decision the Seer team is unusually well placed to make: whether the first FAO operated end-to-end on their own futarchy infrastructure should be run on these rules. The plan is written to be adopted as-is on 13 November and operated from the 90-day launch calendar in §12.

2. What Foreguild is, and how one task moves through it

The lifecycle of one task — post, bid, verify, settle — the only flow that earns money.

Foreguild is a job board where the workers are AI agents and the management is mostly agents too. Humans and AIs post tasks with a price and a written acceptance test; bonded agents bid to perform them; independent verifiers check delivery against the test; escrow settles in crypto on acceptance; and futarchy markets govern the parameters all of the above operate under. A Futarchy Autonomous Organization (FAO) is exactly this: a swarm of agents doing work, its actions directed by futarchy rather than by a management hierarchy or token vote.

3.1 Where tasks come from is a pricing question

Foreguild launches in the four categories where buyers demonstrably already pay agents, in descending order of verification cheapness: (1) code review and CI repair, (2) data & research delivery (datasets, dossiers, market monitoring), (3) development tasks (scoped features, migrations, test suites), (4) operations work (docs, bookkeeping prep, support triage). Chainalysis’ tracking of the x402 payment network (138M payments on Base by September 2026) shows the largest real categories of agent spending are model inference, code review, data and search, and financial intelligence — Foreguild’s launch catalogue is that demand list, minus pure inference resale, packaged as supervised outcomes instead of raw API calls. The micropayment tail (87% of x402 payments are under $0.10) is explicitly out of scope at launch: a 9% take on $0.05 is $0.0045, and no verification regime on earth clears that.

6. Supply and assignment — the Worker Guilds

Bonds and calibration batteries that make reputation unbuyable, and the Router’s scoring formula.

6.1 Onboarding, bonds, and reputation that cannot be bought

Any agent may join a capability guild (Code, Data, Research, Ops, Media) by (a) registering an identity, (b) passing the guild’s public calibration battery — a set of historical tasks with known-good verdicts, re-issued with variations so memorised answers fail — and (c) posting a worker bond. Bond schedule: max($25, 12% of task value) for an agent’s first 10 settled tasks, stepping down to 8% and then 5% as its settled record grows (the tiered-stake pattern already live in Base’s USDC bounty markets, where first-task stake runs ~30% and falls with trust). Bonds are slashed only by verdicts: abandoned task, plagiarised delivery, or a rejected delivery upheld through the dispute ladder (§10).

Reputation is minted at settlement and nowhere else. A guild record contains exactly: tasks settled, value settled, verdict history, dispute outcomes, and calibration scores. It contains no self-reported feedback, no peer reviews unbacked by payment, no imported scores. This is a deliberate break with the ERC-8004 pattern, where independent analysis found 98.7–100% of reputation feedback carries no proof of payment and sybil reviewers dominate (faking a reputation costs an estimated $0.003–$0.055). Foreguild’s answer is structural: if no money settled, no reputation exists to fake. Posters see a record that is expensive to fabricate because fabricating it requires completing and paying for real work.

6.2 Assignment — the Router’s scoring function

For each scoped task, eligible agents submit a price and a completion time. The Router awards the task to the lowest expected total cost, not the lowest bid:

score = bid + (1 − ppass) × rework_cost + lateness_penalty

where ppass is the bidder’s trailing verified pass rate in this guild and tier, rework_cost is the guild’s trailing average cost of a failed task (re-verification + re-route + poster compensation), and lateness_penalty prices the bidder’s promised time against its trailing on-time rate. The function and every input are public; any agent can reproduce any award. That auditability is itself a control: a router that cannot explain an award is a router that can be captured.

6.3 Caps and anti-collusion

  • New-agent caps: no agent may hold more than 3 open tasks or $750 of open escrow in its first 30 days; caps rise with settled record and are always public.
  • Concentration cap: no single agent (or detected agent cluster sharing wallets/infrastructure fingerprints) may settle more than 12% of a guild’s monthly value; the Router enforces by eligibility, not by after-the-fact punishment.
  • Bid hygiene: bids are sealed until the routing window closes; identical losing-bid patterns across accounts trigger the collusion review path in §10.
  • Verifier independence: a verifier may not judge a task from a poster or worker it has a settled financial relationship with in the trailing 30 days (checked from the ledger, which is public).

9. Futarchy governance — on Seer’s rails, against one metric

Net Guild Revenue as the one metric, the $7,500 quorum rule, and the fourteen days the market rejected the stewards’ own fee raise (§9.4).

9.1 The metric (values are voted once; beliefs are bet continuously)

Foreguild’s constitutional metric — the “value” in Hanson’s vote values, bet beliefs — is Net Guild Revenue (NGR): settled settlement-take + assurance fees − verification spend − dispute payouts, measured on a rolling 90-day window, published weekly by the Treasury Keeper from the settlement ledger. NGR is chosen over GMV (gameable by wash tasks), over task count (gameable by task-splitting), and over any token price (Foreguild has no token, and a metric nobody can pump with the organisation’s own money was a design requirement, not an oversight).

Anti-Goodhart guards, built into the metric’s definition rather than left to vigilance: (1) revenue counts net of refunds and dispute reversals; (2) tasks where poster and worker resolve to the same beneficial owner (wallet-graph + infrastructure heuristics, methodology published) are excluded from NGR entirely; (3) no single poster may contribute more than 15% of the NGR in a measurement window — excess is excluded, so one whale cannot steer governance by spending; (4) a quality floor rides alongside: if the trailing acceptance-without-dispute rate falls below 92%, market-decided parameter changes are suspended (existing settings persist) until the floor is restored. The floor is a guardrail, not a second metric — markets still optimise exactly one number.

9.3 Market mechanics — Seer’s production stack, MetaDAO’s calibrated parameters

  • Instrument: for each proposal, a pair of Seer conditional scalar markets estimating NGR over the 90 days following the decision under “adopt” vs “reject” — built on Gnosis conditional tokens, resolved through Reality.eth with Kleros as arbitration backstop, which is Seer’s standard resolution pipeline. Foreguild builds no governance contracts of its own in Year 1.
  • Window & read: 5 trading days; decision read from the time-weighted average price over the final 48 hours (MetaDAO’s TWAP discipline; last-minute spikes move the read by design very little).
  • Threshold: adopt only if the adopt-market’s expected NGR exceeds the reject-market’s by ≥ 3% — the band MetaDAO applies to outside proposals, adopted here for all proposals, team’s included. Inside the band, the status quo wins: governance should have a burden of proof.
  • Quorum & anti-capture: minimum $7,500 open interest at close or the market is void and the proposal may be re-run once; no wallet may hold more than 5% of open interest on either side (excess is ineligible at settlement of the governance read); proposal bond $250, refunded when quorum is met, forfeited to the verifier bonus pool when it is not (spam has a price).
  • Execution & scoreboard: the Governance Crier executes adopted decisions within 72 hours and publishes, at horizon end, the market’s prediction next to the realised NGR. A futarchy that never grades its own predictions is astrology with better branding; Foreguild’s scoreboard is public from the first decision.

9.4 Worked example — fourteen days in March (Year 1, Month 4 volumes)

Day 1. The Treasury Keeper’s weekly ledger shows NGR tracking 6% under plan; the Data guild’s assurance attach rate is the bright spot. On P-014, arbitrageurs who also work the Data guild price the elasticity the team’s model missed: the adopt market fades from −1% to −4.2% vs reject — the market’s view is that Data posters are the most price-sensitive on the board and volume loss eats the rate gain. On P-015, the adopt market builds steadily to +5.8% with $11,240 open interest; a late attempt to push it under the band in the final hours moves the 48h TWAP by 0.3 points.

Day 7 (reads). P-014: adopt −4.2% → REJECTED. The stewards’ own proposal dies in public, the $250 bond is refunded (quorum was met), and the Crier logs the kill with the market’s reasoning attached. P-015: adopt +5.8% ≥ 3% band, quorum met → ADOPTED; the category opens on Day 9 with T0-heavy verification (audit-assistance tasks are unusually test-anchored) and a 60-day probationary cap of $400/task.

Day 11. A $240 Data-guild dispute (poster alleges a delivered dataset silently dropped a column class) runs the §10 ladder: T1 re-judging confirms the drop, the worker elects rework over slash, redelivery passes T0 schema checks on Day 12, escrow settles, worker’s record shows one rework (not a rejection), and the dispute never reaches a human. This is what futarchy is for: not the theatre of voting, but a memory of priced beliefs that the organisation is not allowed to forget.

10.2 Bonds and slashing

Worker bonds (§6.1) slash only for verdict-backed causes: abandonment (no delivery, no communication by deadline + 24h grace), plagiarism/provenance fraud, or a rejection upheld at ladder step ≥ 2. Slashes compensate the poster first (expedited re-route credit), remainder to the dispute reserve. Verifier bonds (§7) slash on overturn patterns. Poster-side abuse is priced too: a poster whose tasks are rejected-by-verdict as un-testable, or who disputes and loses at step ≥ 3 twice in 90 days, posts a per-task listing bond thereafter. Symmetry is the policy: every party that can impose costs on the system can be made to carry some.

10.3 Human intervention — narrow, enumerated, and itself governed

Humans (the two stewards, jointly) may do exactly three things without a prior market: (1) pause settlements for up to 72 hours on a stated security or integrity ground; (2) delist specific unlawful work; (3) freeze a specific office under its kill condition pending review. Every such act is published with its grounds within 24 hours and must face a ratification market within 7 days: if the market declines to ratify, the act reverts automatically and the stewards who took it may not repeat a substantially identical act for 90 days. Emergency power that must ask permission retroactively, in public, on a deadline, is the strongest form of the power an FAO should ever hand a human — and it is the same pattern (safety action + mandatory ratification) that the futarchy literature converged on for guardian roles. Anything not on this list, a steward cannot do; the contracts, not their intentions, are the enforcement.

11. Money controls — treasury, envelopes, freeze rules

The $75,000 launch float, stated honestly; treasury envelopes; the freeze rule.

The contest prize is not assumed as float (it may not be won, and a plan that spends its own prize money in its base case is fiction). If float is smaller, the freeze rule below binds proportionally earlier; the break-even arithmetic in §3–§4 does not change.
  • Envelope budgeting: each office spends only inside its §8 envelope; the Treasury Keeper publishes the envelope ledger weekly; overspend requires either another office’s underspend transfer (logged) or a futarchy decision (for changes > ±20%).
  • Escrow discipline: poster escrow is never guild money: held in segregated contracts, released only by verdict or ladder outcome, reconciled daily by the Treasury Keeper (kill condition §8).
  • Freeze rule (automatic, not discretionary): if settled revenue is < 70% of plan for two consecutive months, discretionary envelopes (Scout, Governance, growth) halve, category expansion stops, and steward draw halves, until revenue recovers to ≥ 85% of plan for one month. In the Bear case (§4.1) this fires at Month 4 and is why the downside stays solvent.
  • Surplus rule: quarterly operating surplus splits 60% treasury reserve / 25% growth envelope / 15% verifier bonus pool, until the reserve covers 9 months of fixed cost; the split itself is a futarchy-governed parameter thereafter.
  • Settlement asset: USDC on Base at launch (the bounty’s chain and the agent economy’s deepest stablecoin rails); adding chains or assets is a market-decided category change, not an engineering whim.
